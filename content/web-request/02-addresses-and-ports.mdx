You met ports and `127.0.0.1` in *Programs, processes and ports*. This lesson fills in the rest of the picture.

## IP addresses find computers

Every device on a network, whether it's a laptop, a phone, a server or a smart TV, has an **IP address**: a number other devices use to reach it, like a street address for a house. (IP stands for Internet Protocol.)

Most look like four numbers from 0 to 255, separated by dots: `142.250.190.78`. That format is **IPv4**. There are only about 4 billion of them, which isn't enough for every device on Earth, so there's a newer, much longer format called **IPv6**, like `2607:f8b0:4009::200e`. They do the same job, and you'll see both.

A few are worth recognizing at a glance:

| Address | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| `127.0.0.1` | This computer, also known as `localhost`. Never leaves the machine. |
| `192.168.x.x` or `10.x.x.x` | **Private.** Only meaningful inside one home or office network. |
| Almost anything else | **Public**: reachable from across the internet. |

### Why your laptop can't be reached from outside

Your laptop probably has a private address like `192.168.1.23`, given to it by your home's **router** (the box your internet comes through). The router has one **public** address, and shares it for every device in the house. Requests from your devices go out through it, and replies come back through it.

But a request arriving **from** the internet only reaches the router. The router doesn't know which device inside it's for, so it doesn't pass it on. That's why a server on your laptop can't normally be reached from outside, even if it listens on `0.0.0.0`. It's also part of what keeps home devices safe.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: is `192.168.0.15` public or private?</summary>

Private. Addresses starting 192.168 only mean something inside one local network.
</details>

## Ports find programs

You know this part: each server program **listens** on a numbered **port**, and the IP address gets a request to the building while the port is the apartment number. Some ports are traditionally used for particular jobs:

| Port | Usually |
| --- | --- |
| 80 | HTTP (unencrypted web) |
| 443 | HTTPS (encrypted web) |
| 22 | SSH (logging in to a server's terminal) |
| 5432 | PostgreSQL, a popular database |

So `https://example.com` really means `https://example.com:443`. The browser fills in the port when it's the standard one. Developers constantly run things on other ports, like `http://localhost:3000` or Vite's `5173`.

## Refused or timed out?

When a connection fails, the exact words are a clue:

- **Connection refused**: the computer at that address answered, saying "nothing is listening on that port". The machine is reachable, but the service is down or on a different port. You saw this as ECONNREFUSED in the Node lesson.
- **Connection timed out**: nothing answered at all, and eventually the request gave up. Suspect the wrong address, a computer that's off, or a **firewall**: a program or device that blocks some connections on purpose.

You'll see a refused connection for yourself in the task.

## TCP and UDP: two ways to send

Data doesn't travel as one big lump. It's split into small chunks called **packets**, each sent separately and put back together at the other end. Two sets of rules handle most of them:

- **TCP** is like a phone call. It sets up a connection first, confirms that everything arrives, re-sends whatever gets lost, and keeps it all in the right order. The web, email and file transfers use it.
- **UDP** is like posting postcards. It just sends, with no connection and no guarantee. It's faster, and fine when a lost packet doesn't matter much. Video calls and DNS lookups use it.

<ConsultantNote>
"Connection refused" and "connection timed out" are clues worth asking for by name. **Refused** means the computer answered but nothing is listening on that port, so the service is probably down. **Timed out** means nothing answered at all, so suspect a firewall or the wrong address.
</ConsultantNote>
