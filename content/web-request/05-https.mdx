## Plain HTTP is a postcard

With plain HTTP, every computer between you and the server can read and change your messages: the café's Wi-Fi, your internet provider, anyone along the path. Passwords and all.

**HTTPS** is HTTP inside an encrypted tunnel. The encryption itself is called **TLS** (you may also hear the older name, SSL). The requests and responses you learned last lesson are unchanged. They're just unreadable to anyone in between.

## The two jobs of HTTPS

1. **Privacy:** nobody in between can read or alter the conversation.
2. **Identity:** you really are talking to `bank.example`, and not someone pretending to be it.

Both matter. Privacy alone isn't enough: an encrypted chat with an attacker is still a chat with an attacker.

## Certificates: the ID card

A **certificate** is a small file saying "this server is `bank.example`", signed by a **Certificate Authority (CA)**, an organization that browsers are built to trust. Your browser checks three things:

- Is it for the exact name in the address bar?
- Has it expired?
- Is it signed by an authority I trust?

If any answer is no, you get a full-page warning.

Most certificates last 90 days or less, because they're renewed automatically. When that automation quietly breaks, a site suddenly shows scary warnings to every visitor. Forgetting to renew is one of the most common causes of outages.

<Callout kind="warn" title="What a padlock doesn't mean">
A padlock means the connection is private and the name matches. It doesn't mean the business is honest. Anyone can get a certificate for `totally-real-bank.example`.
</Callout>

<ConsultantNote>
A quick audit question for any client: "Who gets the alert if a certificate fails to renew?" If nobody knows, you've found a real risk in thirty seconds. Also, check that their site redirects plain `http://` to `https://`.
</ConsultantNote>
