## In this lesson

Consultants get pulled into "the internet is down" more often than you'd think, even when it's not their job. This lesson is the **just enough IT** you need to handle it calmly:

- What the boxes in a home or office actually do: the **modem** and the **router**.
- How your computer gets its address automatically (**DHCP**), and what a **default gateway** is.
- Four commands: **`ipconfig`**, **`ping`**, **`tracert`** and **`Test-NetConnection`**.
- A step-by-step **troubleshooting ladder** that finds where a network problem is.

## The boxes on the shelf

Most homes and small offices have one or two boxes with blinking lights. They do two different jobs, even when they're built into one box:

- The **modem** connects your building to your **internet provider** (the company you pay for internet) over a phone line, cable or fiber. It's the door to the outside world.
- The **router** runs your **local network**: all the devices inside the building. In *Addresses and ports* you saw that it shares one public IP address between all your devices (NAT). It also hands out addresses, which you'll see in a moment.

Most routers also provide Wi-Fi. Devices connect either by **Wi-Fi** (radio, convenient but slower and less reliable through walls) or by **Ethernet** (a network cable, faster and steadier). When a video call keeps freezing, "can you plug in a cable?" is a classic first step.

## How your laptop gets an address

When your laptop joins a network, it doesn't know its own IP address yet. So it shouts to the whole network: "I'm new, can someone give me an address?" The router answers with:

- an **IP address** for the laptop to use, like `192.168.1.23`, a private address,
- the **default gateway**: the router's own address, like `192.168.1.1`. Anything the laptop wants to send outside the local network goes there first,
- which **DNS servers** to use for looking up names. Usually the router itself or the provider's, and sometimes a public one like `1.1.1.1`.

This automatic hand-out is called **DHCP** (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol). It's why you never type an address when you join a Wi-Fi network.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The 169.254 warning sign">
If a Windows computer shows an address starting `169.254`, no DHCP server answered, so Windows made up a placeholder address. It can't reach anything. Check the Wi-Fi connection or the cable, or restart the router.
</Callout>

<details>
<summary>Quick check: what is the default gateway?</summary>

The router's address on your local network. Anything going outside the network is sent there first.
</details>

## ipconfig: what does my computer think?

`ipconfig` shows your computer's network settings. A trimmed example:

```text
Wireless LAN adapter Wi-Fi:

   IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.23
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.1
```

- **Adapter**: the piece of hardware, here the Wi-Fi. You may also see Ethernet, and some virtual ones you can ignore.
- **IPv4 Address**: this computer's address on the local network.
- **Subnet Mask**: which addresses count as "local". `255.255.255.0` means any address starting `192.168.1.` is in the same network and can be reached directly. You rarely need more than that.
- **Default Gateway**: the router.

Two handy variations: `ipconfig /all` also shows the DNS servers, and `ipconfig /flushdns` throws away the DNS answers your computer has saved, which helps after a website has moved.

## ping: are you there?

`ping` sends a tiny "are you there?" message and times the answer:

```text
> ping -n 3 192.168.1.1

Reply from 192.168.1.1: bytes=32 time=2ms TTL=64
Reply from 192.168.1.1: bytes=32 time=1ms TTL=64
Reply from 192.168.1.1: bytes=32 time=3ms TTL=64
```

- `-n 3` means "send three". (On Mac and Linux it's `-c 3`, and without it ping keeps going until you press Ctrl+C.)
- **time** is the round trip in milliseconds. A few ms is nearby. Across an ocean, 100ms or more is normal.
- **TTL** ("time to live") counts down by one at every router a packet passes, so a lost packet can't circle forever. You can ignore the number.

"Request timed out" means no answer came back.

<Callout kind="warn" title="No ping reply doesn't mean it's down">
Many servers and firewalls ignore ping on purpose. If a website doesn't answer ping, test the service itself before you conclude anything, as you'll see in a moment.
</Callout>

## tracert: which way did it go?

`tracert` lists every router your packets pass through on the way to a destination, one per line:

```text
> tracert -d 1.1.1.1

  1     2 ms     1 ms     2 ms  192.168.1.1
  2    11 ms     9 ms    10 ms  100.64.0.1
  3    14 ms    12 ms    13 ms  203.0.113.9
  ...
  8    15 ms    14 ms    15 ms  1.1.1.1
```

Line 1 is your own router. The next few are your provider. `-d` skips looking up names for each step, which makes it much faster. If the list stops at a certain point and only shows `*` after it, that's roughly where things break. (Like ping, some routers don't answer, so a few `*` lines in the middle are normal.)

## Test-NetConnection: is the service answering?

A web server can be perfectly healthy while ignoring ping. What you really want to know is: **is something listening on the port?** PowerShell's `Test-NetConnection` tries to open a connection to a port:

```powershell
Test-NetConnection shop.example -Port 443
```

The line to look for is `TcpTestSucceeded : True`. If it's `False`, you saw this in *Addresses and ports*: either the connection was **refused** (the computer answered, but nothing listens on that port) or it **timed out** (nothing answered, often a firewall).

In the task you'll write `(Test-NetConnection 127.0.0.1 -Port 4314).TcpTestSucceeded`. The round brackets run the command first, and `.TcpTestSucceeded` picks out just the True or False from its answer, the same dot you used to read values in *Settings files*.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: a server ignores ping, but Test-NetConnection to port 443 says True. Is the website up?</summary>

The web server is at least listening and accepting connections, so very probably yes. The ping result just means ping is blocked.
</details>

## The troubleshooting ladder

When something "doesn't work", don't guess. Climb this ladder from the inside out. **The first step that fails is where the problem is.**

| Step | Ask | Command | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Is it everyone, or one device? | Ask around | One device: focus on that device |
| 2 | Does this device have an address? | `ipconfig` | No address, or 169.254: Wi-Fi, cable or DHCP problem |
| 3 | Can it reach the router? | `ping 192.168.1.1` (the gateway) | Local network problem: Wi-Fi, cable or router |
| 4 | Can it reach the internet by IP address? | `ping 1.1.1.1` | Router, modem or internet provider problem |
| 5 | Do names work? | `nslookup example.com` | DNS problem. Try `ipconfig /flushdns`, or check the DNS settings |
| 6 | Does the service answer? | `Test-NetConnection site -Port 443`, or the browser | That service is down, or a firewall blocks it |

This is the same idea as the **five stages** from *The trip a web request takes*, seen from the network's side. Once you know which step fails, you know who to call: the office's IT person, the internet provider, the DNS host, or whoever runs the website.

<ConsultantNote>
You'll often be the most technical person a small client knows, so "is the internet down?" lands on you even when you were hired for something else. Answering with "let's find out which step fails" rather than guessing makes you look calm and competent. It also produces evidence, like "the router answers but nothing beyond it does", that makes a call to the internet provider short and productive.
</ConsultantNote>
