## In this lesson

In *Addresses and ports* you met a **firewall** as "something that blocks some connections on purpose", the usual reason a connection times out. This lesson looks at how they decide, and at **VPNs**, which are often sold as security but do something quite specific:

- **Firewall rules**: inbound and outbound, ports, sources, and **default deny**.
- Firewalls on your own computer and around whole networks, including the cloud's **security groups**.
- **Network segmentation**: public and private zones.
- **VPNs**: the company kind and the consumer kind, and what each really protects.
- **Zero trust**, the idea that's replacing "inside the network means safe".

## How a firewall decides

A firewall checks each connection against a list of **rules**, in order. A rule says, roughly:

> **Allow** (or deny) connections **coming in** to port **443**, **from** any address.

Each rule looks at a few things:

- **Direction**: **inbound** (someone connecting to you) or **outbound** (you connecting out).
- **Port**: which service, like 443 for HTTPS or 22 for SSH.
- **Source**: which addresses it applies to. "Any", or a range like the office network, `10.0.0.something`.
- **Action**: allow or deny.

The first rule that matches decides. And the most important rule of all is the one at the end: **if nothing matched, deny**. This is **default deny**. You list exactly what should get through, so anything you forgot is blocked rather than accidentally open.

A typical web server's inbound rules:

| # | Direction | Port | From | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | In | 443 | Any | Allow |
| 2 | In | 22 | 10.0.0.x (office) | Allow |
| (end) | In | anything | anything | **Deny** |

In the task you'll write exactly this decision in JavaScript.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: with the table above, can someone at home connect to port 22?</summary>

No. Rule 2 only allows the office's addresses, so default deny blocks them. (They'd use the VPN, coming up.)
</details>

## A firewall when it blocks: silence

Firewalls usually block by **dropping** the packet silently, rather than answering "no". That's why a blocked connection **hangs and times out**, while a port with nothing listening gives an instant **connection refused**. Silence tells an attacker nothing, not even that the computer exists.

## Where firewalls live

- **On your own computer.** Windows has a built-in firewall. When a program first tries to listen on the network, Windows may ask "Allow access?" That's this firewall adding an inbound rule. (Groundwork listens only on `127.0.0.1`, which the firewall doesn't need to open, so it never asks.)
- **Around a network.** Office routers and dedicated firewall boxes filter traffic for everything behind them.
- **In the cloud.** Every cloud server sits behind rules you configure, called **security groups** in AWS and **network security groups** in Azure. They're firewalls managed through a web page.

A firewall on one computer is a **host firewall**, and one guarding a whole network is a **network firewall**. Real systems use both.

## Segmentation: not everything belongs on the internet

Think back to the load balancer design: only the load balancer faced the internet, and the app servers and database were on a **private network**. That's **network segmentation**: splitting a system into zones, so each part is only reachable by what genuinely needs it.

- **Public zone**: the website, the load balancer, the VPN gateway. Only on the ports they need.
- **Private zone**: databases, admin tools, internal services. Unreachable from the internet at all.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The breaches that happen every week">
Databases, remote desktop (port 3389) and admin panels left open to the internet "just for now" are among the most common causes of real break-ins. Automated scanners sweep the whole internet constantly, and they find an open database port within hours. If a developer needs access from home, that's what a VPN is for.
</Callout>

## VPNs: a private road through a public network

A **VPN** (virtual private network) creates an **encrypted tunnel** from your device to a VPN server. Everything you send goes through the tunnel, comes out at the VPN server, and continues from there. Anyone in between, like the café's Wi-Fi or your internet provider, sees only scrambled data going to the VPN server.

There are two quite different uses.

### The company VPN

A **corporate VPN** lets staff outside the office reach the company's **private network**, as if they were plugged in at their desk. The admin panel and database stay off the internet, and staff connect to the VPN gateway first. That's the design in the task.

Often only traffic for company systems goes through the tunnel, and everything else, like YouTube, goes straight to the internet. This is **split tunneling**. It's faster, but the company can't see or filter that other traffic.

### The consumer VPN

A **consumer VPN** (the kind advertised on podcasts) sends **all** your traffic through the VPN company's servers. What it really does:

| It does | It doesn't |
| --- | --- |
| Hide which sites you visit from the café's Wi-Fi and your internet provider | Make you anonymous: sites you log in to still know who you are |
| Make sites see the VPN server's IP address instead of yours | Stop viruses, phishing or weak passwords |
| Help on untrusted Wi-Fi | Hide anything from the VPN company, which now sees your traffic |

Notice that HTTPS already hides **what** you send to each site. A consumer VPN mainly hides **which** sites you connect to, and from whom. It moves your trust from the local network to the VPN company. Sometimes that's a good trade, but it isn't "security" in general.

## Zero trust

The old model was a castle with a moat: everything inside the network (or on the VPN) is trusted, everything outside isn't. The trouble is that once an attacker gets inside, through one stolen password or one infected laptop, they can reach everything.

**Zero trust** flips that: **don't trust a connection just because of where it comes from**. Check who the user is, and whether their device is healthy, for **each** app, on **every** request. Tools like Cloudflare Access, Microsoft Entra and Tailscale do this, often replacing the traditional VPN. You'll come back to it in Stage 4's security lessons.
