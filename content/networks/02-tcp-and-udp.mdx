## In this lesson

In *Addresses and ports* you met **TCP** (reliable, like a phone call) and **UDP** (fast, no guarantees, like postcards). This lesson opens them up:

- How TCP sets up a connection: the **three-way handshake**.
- How it makes delivery reliable with **sequence numbers** and **retransmission**.
- Why UDP throws all of that away on purpose, and which apps want that.
- **Latency** and **bandwidth**: why a "faster internet" sometimes doesn't make a website any faster.

## The network is messy

Data travels in **packets**: small chunks, each with the destination address on it. Each one is passed from router to router, and the network makes no promises:

- Packets can take **different routes** and arrive **out of order**.
- Some get **lost**, for example when a router is too busy or Wi-Fi drops for a moment. This is called **packet loss**.
- Occasionally one arrives **twice**.

That's fine for some things and disastrous for others. A web page, a photo or a bank payment must arrive complete and in order. TCP's whole job is to build that guarantee on top of a network that doesn't offer it.

## Step 1: the handshake

Before any real data moves, TCP sets up a **connection**. Step through it:

<Viz name="tcp-handshake" />

1. The client sends **SYN** ("synchronize"): "Can we talk? I'll number my packets starting from 100."
2. The server answers **SYN-ACK**: "Yes. I got your 100, and I'll number mine from 300."
3. The client sends **ACK** ("acknowledge"): "Agreed."

This is the **three-way handshake**. It costs one full **round trip**: there and back again. Now you know what **connection refused** looks like from the inside: the computer answered the SYN with "nothing is listening on that port".

With HTTPS, the encryption handshake you saw in the HTTPS lesson comes **after** this one, so a secure connection costs at least two round trips before the first byte of your page is even requested.

## Step 2: numbering and resending

Every packet TCP sends has a **sequence number**, like page numbers on a long letter. The receiver:

- puts packets back in order using the numbers,
- throws away any repeats,
- regularly confirms ("ACKs") how far it has received **everything** without a gap.

If a packet goes missing, the receiver keeps confirming only up to the gap. The sender notices that packet was never confirmed and sends it again. That's **retransmission**. The program at the other end never sees any of this: it just receives the data, complete and in order.

The cost: while TCP waits for the missing packet, everything after it waits too, even if it already arrived. Packet loss doesn't cause a gap. It causes a **delay**. That's why a bad connection makes web pages hang rather than appear with holes.

In the Python task you'll write the receiver's side of this: put packets back in order, ignore repeats, and work out which sequence numbers are missing.

## UDP: no handshake, no promises

**UDP** skips all of it. No handshake, no sequence numbers, no confirmations, no resending. A program hands UDP a packet and it's sent. If it's lost, it's lost.

Why would anyone want that? Because sometimes **late data is worthless**:

| App | Why UDP fits |
| --- | --- |
| Video and voice calls | A sound from half a second ago is useless now. A tiny glitch beats freezing to wait for it. |
| Online games | Only the newest position of each player matters. |
| Live video streams | Keep playing, even if a few packets drop. |
| DNS lookups | One small question and one small answer each fit in a single packet. If no answer comes, just ask again. |

Apps built on UDP add only the parts of reliability they need. And the newest version of HTTP, HTTP/3, runs on top of UDP with its own smarter version of TCP's guarantees. You'll see why in *Faster HTTP*.

## Latency and bandwidth

Two numbers describe a connection, and people mix them up constantly:

- **Bandwidth** is how much data can travel **at once**, in megabits per second (Mbps). It's what internet providers advertise.
- **Latency** is how long one trip **takes**, in milliseconds (ms). The `time=` in ping's output is a round trip.

A motorway makes it clear. Bandwidth is the **number of lanes**. Latency is the **length of the drive**. Adding lanes lets more cars travel at once, but each car's trip takes just as long.

Typical round-trip times:

| From your laptop to… | Round trip |
| --- | --- |
| Your own router | 1–5 ms |
| A server in your own city | 5–20 ms |
| A server across your country | 30–80 ms |
| A server on another continent | 100–300 ms |

Light in fiber is fast, but not infinitely fast. Crossing an ocean and back takes a noticeable fraction of a second, and no amount of money changes physics.

### Why latency usually wins

Loading a web page isn't one big download. It's lots of small back-and-forth trips: DNS, the TCP handshake, the TLS handshake, the page, then the images, styles and scripts it asks for. Each one waits for a round trip.

If a server is 150ms away and a page needs 8 round trips one after another, that's 1.2 seconds of pure waiting, however much bandwidth you have. This is why the big tricks for fast websites are about **fewer round trips** and **shorter distances**: reusing connections (*Faster HTTP*) and keeping copies of files near users (*CDNs*).

<ConsultantNote>
"Our site is slow, should we upgrade our hosting?" is a common client question, and the answer is often no. If their users are in Australia and the server is in Virginia, every round trip costs 200ms or more, and a bigger server doesn't change that. Knowing the difference between latency and bandwidth lets you recommend the fix that actually works, and stops a client from paying for one that doesn't.
</ConsultantNote>
