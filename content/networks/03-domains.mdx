## In this lesson

In *DNS* you learned how a name becomes an IP address. This lesson is DNS from the side of someone who **owns** a domain, which is what your clients will be:

- Who you buy a domain from, and the renewal trap.
- How a domain is **pointed** at a website, with **A** and **CNAME** records.
- **Subdomains** and the **apex**.
- The records behind **email**: **MX**, and the **TXT** records that fight forged email.
- The two mistakes that take small businesses offline, and how to prevent them.

## Buying a domain

A domain name isn't bought forever. It's **rented**, usually a year at a time, from a **registrar**: a company allowed to sell names, like Namecheap, GoDaddy, Cloudflare or Squarespace.

The ending of the name is the **TLD** (top-level domain): `.com`, `.org`, `.uk`, `.dev` and hundreds more. Each TLD is run by one organization, which is why prices differ: a `.com` might cost $10–15 a year, and some fancy endings far more.

When you register a name, the registrar records two important things:

- **Who owns it**, with a contact email. Renewal reminders and important warnings go there.
- **Which name servers** answer DNS questions for it. These are the **authoritative servers** from the DNS lesson. Changing them hands your whole domain's DNS to a different company.

### The renewal trap

If nobody renews it, the domain **expires**. The website and email stop, and after a grace period anyone can buy the name. This happens to real businesses all the time, usually because the reminders went to an ex-employee's email address or an old web designer.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The cheapest insurance there is">
Turn on **auto-renew**, make sure the contact email is one someone actually reads, and know which account the domain lives in. Ask every new client: "Who controls your domain, and when does it renew?" Many don't know.
</Callout>

## Three companies, one name

People often assume the domain, the DNS and the website are all one thing. They're three jobs, and they're often done by three different companies:

| Job | Who does it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Registrar** | Rents you the name | Namecheap |
| **DNS host** | Answers DNS questions with your records | Cloudflare (often the registrar too) |
| **Web host** | Runs the website | GitHub Pages, Vercel, Squarespace |

Knowing which is which is half of fixing any domain problem.

## Pointing a domain at a website

Your DNS host keeps a list of **records**. You met the types in the DNS lesson. Here's how you use the two main ones in practice.

An **A record** points a name straight at an IPv4 address (an **AAAA** record does the same for IPv6):

```text
smiledental.example.      A      203.0.113.10
```

A **CNAME** record says "this name is just another name for that one, so go and look that one up instead":

```text
www.smiledental.example.  CNAME  smiledental.example.
```

Web hosts usually ask for a CNAME, because it lets them change their own IP addresses whenever they like without you touching your DNS. For example, to use your own domain with GitHub Pages, you'd add a CNAME pointing at `yourname.github.io`.

Following a CNAME is exactly what you'll code in the task: look up the name, and if it's a CNAME, look up the name it points to, until you reach an A record.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: a hosting company tells you "point www at shops.hostco.example". Which record do you add?</summary>

A CNAME for `www` with the value `shops.hostco.example`.
</details>

## Subdomains and the apex

Anything you put in front of the domain is a **subdomain**: `www.smiledental.example`, `bookings.smiledental.example`, `staging.smiledental.example`. You can make as many as you like, for free, by adding records. Each can point somewhere completely different: the bookings subdomain might point at a booking company's servers.

The bare name, `smiledental.example`, is called the **apex** (or "root" or "naked" domain). It has one quirk: it **can't be a CNAME**, because of how DNS rules work. So the apex usually gets an A record, or a special alias record that some DNS hosts offer. A very common setup is: the apex has an A record, and `www` is a CNAME pointing at the apex.

## The records behind email

Email finds its way using DNS too, with completely separate records from the website.

**MX** ("mail exchanger") records say which servers receive email for the domain:

```text
smiledental.example.      MX     10 mail.protection.example.
```

The number is a priority: lower is tried first, so a domain can have backup mail servers. If a domain has **no** MX records, email to it has nowhere to go.

**TXT** records hold free text. Two big uses:

1. **Proving you own a domain.** A service (Google, Microsoft, a newsletter tool) gives you a code to add as a TXT record. Only the domain's owner can add records, so finding it proves you're in control.
2. **Stopping forged email.** Anyone can write "From: boss@smiledental.example" on an email. Three TXT-based standards let receiving mail servers check:
   - **SPF** lists which servers are allowed to send email for the domain. It starts `v=spf1`.
   - **DKIM** publishes a key used to check a signature on each email.
   - **DMARC** tells receivers what to do when a check fails, like "send it to spam".

Together, these **email authentication records** decide whether a client's invoices land in the inbox or the spam folder. Big email providers now reject bulk email from domains without them.

<Callout title="in works on text too">
In the Python lessons you used `in` to check whether a key is in a dictionary or an item is in a list. It also works on **text**: `"v=spf1" in "v=spf1 include:mail.example -all"` is `True`, because the first text appears somewhere inside the second. You'll use it in the task to spot SPF records.
</Callout>

## Looking at a real domain's records

When you're online, `nslookup` can ask for any record type with `-type=`:

```powershell
nslookup -type=MX gmail.com
nslookup -type=TXT gmail.com
```

This is the first thing to check when email stops arriving, or when a client says "I added the record but it doesn't work". Remember **TTL** from the DNS lesson: a change can take a while to reach everyone, so check the records at the source (the DNS host's dashboard) as well as what your own computer sees.

## The two disasters

Almost every domain emergency in a small business is one of these:

1. **The domain expired.** Prevent it with auto-renew and a monitored contact email.
2. **Records were lost in a move.** Someone changed the **name servers** to a new DNS host, recreated the website's records there, and forgot the MX and TXT records. The website works, so nobody notices for days that no email is arriving. Prevent it by **keeping a copy of every record** before any DNS change, and checking them all afterwards.

<ConsultantNote>
Early in any engagement, write down the client's whole domain setup: registrar and renewal date, who has the login, the DNS host, every record, and where the website and email live. It takes half an hour, it often uncovers a near-disaster (like the domain being in an ex-employee's personal account), and it makes you the person who knows how everything is connected.
</ConsultantNote>
