## In this lesson

The first lesson gave you the routine for when you're stuck: try it yourself, ask for a hint, finish it yourself, explain it back. This lesson is about everything **around** that: how to make what you learn actually stay in your head, using an AI assistant as a study partner.

You'll learn:

- Why answering questions from memory beats re-reading, and how to get an AI to quiz you properly.
- **Explaining back**: the fastest way to find the holes in your own understanding.
- How to ask for a **different explanation** when a lesson doesn't click, and why you still check it.
- Keeping a short **learning log**.
- What to do when you've been stuck **too long**.

## Remembering is a workout

Think about the last time you read something, nodded along, and then couldn't remember it a week later. That's normal. Reading puts ideas in front of you, but it doesn't make you **use** them.

What does work is pulling an idea **out** of your memory: answering a question with the lesson closed. It feels harder than re-reading, and that's the point. Every time you dig an idea out of memory, it gets easier to find the next time, a bit like a path through grass that gets clearer each time someone walks it. This is called **retrieval practice**, and it's one of the best-proven ways to learn anything.

You've already been doing it. Groundwork's **Recall** step, where you answer from memory with no notes, is retrieval practice. So are the review cards that come back days later. An AI assistant lets you do more of it, on anything, whenever you want.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: re-reading a lesson or answering questions about it with the lesson closed. Which builds memory better?</summary>

Answering with the lesson closed. Re-reading makes ideas feel familiar, but pulling them out of memory is what makes them stick.
</details>

## Getting an AI to quiz you properly

Asking "quiz me on DNS" usually gets you a list of ten questions with the answers right underneath. That's re-reading with extra steps: your eyes slide to the answer before your brain has done any work.

A good quiz prompt sets a few rules:

```text
I'm a beginner and I've just finished a lesson on files and folders.
Quiz me on it. Ask ONE question at a time and wait for my answer.
Don't show the answer until I've replied. Then tell me if I was
right and explain why in a sentence or two. Start easy, get harder.
At the end, ask me again about the ones I got wrong, worded differently.
```

Each line has a job:

- **"I'm a beginner … a lesson on files and folders"** keeps the questions at the right level and on the right topic. You can paste in the lesson's "Words in this lesson" table to keep it focused.
- **"One question at a time … don't show the answer"** forces you to remember instead of recognize.
- **"Explain why"** turns a wrong answer into a mini-lesson, not just a red cross.
- **"Ask again … worded differently"** makes sure you understood the idea, not just memorized one sentence.

### Mix in older topics

Add one more line: *"Also include one question about something I learned last week, like the terminal."* Ideas you never revisit fade. Mixing topics also makes you work out **which** idea a question needs, which is exactly what real problems ask of you. Mixing topics like this is called **interleaving**.

<Callout title="Answer for real">
Type your actual answer, even if you're unsure. "I think it's something about the folder above?" is a perfectly good answer. A guess that's wrong, then corrected, sticks better than no guess at all. That's also why every Groundwork lesson starts with Predict.
</Callout>

## Explaining back

Here's a test of whether you really understand something: can you explain it in plain words to someone who's never heard of it? Wherever you get vague, wave your hands or say "it just sort of works", there's a hole in your understanding.

AI is a great audience for this, because it can push back. Try:

```text
I'm going to explain what a file path is, in my own words, as if to a
friend who doesn't use computers much. Then point out anything I got
wrong or left vague, and ask me two follow-up questions that test
whether I really understand it.
```

Then write your explanation **without looking at the lesson**. The follow-up questions are the valuable part: they find the edge of what you know. When you hit a question you can't answer, you've found exactly what to go back and re-read. This is **explaining back**.

Some Groundwork lessons have an "Explain it to a client" task that works the same way: you write an explanation, score it against a checklist, and you can paste it into an AI for feedback. Being able to explain technical ideas simply is a big part of a consultant's job, so this is practice for the work itself, not just for learning.

<details>
<summary>Quick check: when you're explaining back, which part shows you what to study next?</summary>

The places where you get vague or stuck, and the follow-up questions you can't answer. That's where the gaps are.
</details>

## When a lesson doesn't click: ask for another angle

Sometimes you read an explanation twice and it still doesn't land. That's not a sign you can't learn this. Often the explanation just isn't the right one **for you**. A different example, a different comparison or a simpler version can make it click.

AI is very good at this:

```text
I'm learning about relative paths. This paragraph from my course doesn't
make sense to me: [paste the paragraph]. Can you explain the same idea
a different way, with an everyday example? Keep it short.
```

Other useful versions:

- *"Explain it as if I'm 12."*
- *"Give me three different examples, from simple to realistic."*
- *"What's the most common misunderstanding about this?"*

Asking for a fresh explanation like this is getting **another angle**.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Then check it against the lesson">
A different explanation is still an AI answer, and AI can be confidently wrong. Read the lesson again after the new explanation. If they seem to disagree, trust the lesson or the official documentation, not the AI, and maybe flag the lesson so it can be improved.
</Callout>

## Keep a learning log

A **learning log** is a few lines you write after each study session, in your own words. Nothing fancy:

```text
Tue: Relative paths. ".." means the folder above. Finally get it:
it's like directions from where you're standing, not a full address.
Still unsure: when do I need the full path instead?
```

It takes two minutes and does three jobs:

1. Writing it is itself retrieval practice.
2. The "still unsure" line tells you, or your AI tutor, exactly what to ask next time.
3. Weeks later, you can see how far you've come, which helps a lot on the days it feels slow.

Every Groundwork lesson has a **notes** box, which is a good place for this. You can also paste your log into an AI and ask it to quiz you on everything from the past week.

## Knowing when you're stuck too long

Some struggle is good. It's how you find out where your understanding ends. But past a certain point, struggling stops teaching you anything and just eats your evening.

A simple rule: **if you've made no progress for about 30 minutes, change something.** In order:

1. **Write down exactly what you tried** and what happened. Surprisingly often, the answer appears while you're writing it out.
2. **Ask your AI tutor for a bigger hint**, pasting in what you wrote.
3. **Take a break.** A walk really does help. Your brain keeps working on it.
4. **Ask a person**, and **flag the lesson**. In Groundwork, the "How clear was this?" question and the problem button are there for exactly this. If something is confusing you, it's probably confusing other people too, and the lesson can be fixed.

This **stuck rule** isn't giving up. Professional developers follow it too: they ask a colleague after half an hour, because an hour lost to something a teammate could explain in a minute is a waste.

<ConsultantNote>
These habits don't stop when the course ends. Consultants constantly learn new tools on the job, often under time pressure: a client uses a system you've never seen, and you need to understand it by Thursday. Quizzing yourself, explaining things back and knowing when to ask for help are how you get up to speed fast, and how you avoid confidently telling a client something you only half understand.
</ConsultantNote>
