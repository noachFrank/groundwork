## Confident isn't the same as correct

AI models write by predicting what text is most likely to come next. That makes them fluent, fast and usually helpful. It also means they produce **plausible** text, and a wrong answer can be every bit as plausible as a right one.

So AI doesn't sound unsure when it's wrong. It sounds exactly the same. You can't judge an answer by its tone.

## The usual kinds of wrong

| What happens | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Invented details** | A setting, command option or function that doesn't exist |
| **Out of date** | Instructions for an old version of a tool that has changed since |
| **Almost right** | Code that works for the example but breaks for real data |
| **Silencing the warning** | A "fix" that makes an error go away by turning off the safety check |
| **One small slip** | A typo in one name, inside a script that otherwise makes perfect sense |

The last one is the sneakiest, and you'll find one in the task below.

## Habits that catch it

- **Check the official documentation.** Each Groundwork lesson links to it. If a setting or option isn't there, be suspicious.
- **Test somewhere safe.** Use a practice folder, a copy of the data, or a test account, never the real thing first.
- **Read before you run.** Especially anything that deletes, moves, installs or changes settings.
- **Ask for the why.** "What does each line do?" often makes the AI catch its own mistake, and it always helps you learn.
- **Be wary of unknown packages.** AI sometimes invents package names, and attackers publish harmful packages under exactly those names.

<Callout title="Don't overcorrect">
Most of the time, AI is right, and it's an excellent tool. The point isn't to distrust everything. It's to check in proportion to the risk: a quick look for a harmless print statement, a careful one for anything that touches real files, money or customers.
</Callout>

<ConsultantNote>
When you give a client advice, your name is on it, not the AI's. "The AI said so" is never an acceptable explanation for a mistake. The habit of checking is part of what makes you worth hiring.
</ConsultantNote>
