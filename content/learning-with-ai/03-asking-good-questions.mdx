## The answer can only be as good as the question

Whoever you ask, whether it's an AI, a colleague or a forum, they can't see your screen. All they know is what you tell them. A vague question gets a guess. A clear question usually gets the answer.

## The four things to include

1. **What you're trying to do.** The goal, in a sentence. Sometimes the best answer is "there's an easier way to do that".
2. **What actually happens.** The **exact** error message, copied and pasted, not retyped from memory. Small details in errors matter.
3. **What you've already tried.** This saves time and shows where the problem isn't.
4. **Your setup.** Operating system, versions, how things were installed. "Works on my machine" problems hide here.

If you can, add a **small example**: the fewest lines of code, or the smallest bit of data, that still shows the problem. Making one often reveals the answer before you even ask.

<Callout title="A useful side effect">
Writing a clear question forces you to think clearly about the problem. Programmers call this **rubber duck debugging**: explaining a problem out loud, even to a rubber duck, often makes you spot the answer yourself.
</Callout>

## What never goes in

Anything you paste into an online AI tool is sent to another company's computers. Depending on the tool and its settings, it may be stored or reviewed. So:

- **Never paste secrets.** Passwords, API keys and access tokens get replaced with a placeholder like `YOUR_API_KEY_HERE`.
- **Never paste private data.** Real customers', patients' or employees' details stay out. Use a few rows of **made-up data** in the same format.
- **Check the client's rules.** Many organizations have policies about which AI tools can see their data. Ask.

<ConsultantNote>
Clients trust you with access to their systems and their customers' information. Leaking it, even by accident, even "just to an AI", can break privacy laws like GDPR or HIPAA and end the relationship. Handling data carefully is a skill clients notice.
</ConsultantNote>
