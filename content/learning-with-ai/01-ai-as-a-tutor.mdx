## Before you start

This is the very first lesson, so it assumes nothing. You don't need to know anything about computers or programming yet. If a word is new, it gets explained the first time it appears, and there's a list of every new word at the bottom.

In this lesson you'll learn:

- What an AI assistant is, and what it's good and bad at.
- Why getting answers from AI can **feel** like learning when it isn't.
- A simple routine that makes AI teach you instead of doing the work for you.

## What an AI assistant is

An **AI assistant** is a program you chat with by typing, like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. You type a message, and it types back an answer. It has read a huge amount of text, including a lot of programming help, so it can explain ideas, answer questions and write things for you.

The message you type to it is called a **prompt**. "Explain what a folder is" is a prompt. So is a long message with your own work pasted in. The better your prompt, the better the answer. There's a whole lesson on that later in this module.

Throughout this course you'll also see the word **code**. Code is instructions for a computer, written as text in a special language that a computer can follow. A tiny example:

```python
print("Hello")
```

That's one line of code in a language called Python. It tells the computer to show the word Hello on the screen. You'll learn to read and write code like this later. For now, all you need to know is that code is just text: precise instructions, written for a computer.

## AI is the best tutor you've ever had, if you use it right

You have something learners ten years ago didn't: a patient expert you can ask anything, at any hour, who never gets tired of the same question. Used well, that's an enormous advantage. Used badly, it quietly stops you from learning at all.

The difference comes down to one question: **who is doing the thinking?**

- A **tutor** helps you think. They ask what you tried, point you in the right direction, and let you get there yourself.
- A **ghostwriter** does the work for you and lets you put your name on it.

An AI will happily be either. It's up to you which one you ask for.

## The trap

When an AI hands you a finished answer, you read it, it makes sense, and you feel like you understand. That feeling is misleading.

Here's why. **Following** someone else's reasoning is far easier than **producing** it yourself. It's like watching someone solve a puzzle: every step looks obvious as you watch, but that doesn't mean you could solve the next puzzle alone. Your brain mistakes "I recognize this" for "I can do this".

The test is simple. Close the answer and try the problem again tomorrow, with nothing to look at. If you can't, you didn't learn it. You watched it.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The sign you've fallen into the trap">
You're finishing exercises quickly, but you couldn't explain your own work to a friend.
</Callout>

## The routine

Use these four steps every time you're stuck, whether it's on a Groundwork exercise or anything else:

1. **Try it yourself first.** Even a wrong attempt helps, because it shows you exactly where your understanding runs out. Five honest minutes of trying is enough.
2. **Ask for a hint, not the answer.** Show the AI what you tried and what went wrong, then ask what to look at. For example: *"Here's what I tried and here's what happened. Don't give me the answer. What should I look at?"*
3. **Finish it yourself.** Take the hint and keep going. If you're still stuck, ask for a slightly bigger hint.
4. **Explain it back.** Ask the AI to quiz you, or explain the solution in your own words. If you can't, you're not done.

### Why Groundwork works the same way

Every Groundwork lesson has the same five steps, shown at the top of the page. This is the **learning loop**:

| Step | What you do | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Predict** | Guess the answer before you're taught | A guess, even a wrong one, makes your brain pay attention to the real answer |
| **Learn** | Read the lesson | This is the explanation |
| **Do** | Complete real tasks that get checked | Doing is how skills form |
| **Recall** | Answer from memory, with no notes | Pulling something out of memory is what makes it stick |
| **Review later** | Key ideas come back days and weeks later | Spacing practice out stops you forgetting |

That's on purpose. Each step is something research on learning shows works better than just reading.

## When to let AI just do it

This isn't about refusing help. Working professionals use AI constantly. The rule is:

> **AI does the parts you're not trying to learn. You do the parts you are.**

Some examples:

| Task | Let AI do it? |
| --- | --- |
| Typing out 50 rows of made-up names for practice | Yes, you're not learning to type names |
| Writing your first piece of code that repeats something | No, that's exactly what you're learning |
| Explaining a word you don't understand | Yes, then check you can explain it back |
| Fixing an error in an exercise | Hints only, then fix it yourself |

## Setting up your tutor

Most AI tools let you give **standing instructions**: a message it reads before every conversation. In ChatGPT these are called "custom instructions", and in Claude you can put them in a "project". If your tool doesn't have that, paste them at the start of each chat.

Something like this works well:

```text
I'm a beginner learning to code. Act as my tutor, not my ghostwriter.
When I'm stuck, ask what I've tried and give me the smallest useful hint.
Only give the full answer if I ask twice. Afterwards, quiz me with one
or two questions. If you're unsure about something, say so and point me
to the official documentation.
```

Each line has a job:

- "Act as my tutor, not my ghostwriter" sets the role.
- "Ask what I've tried" makes you do step 1 of the routine.
- "The smallest useful hint" stops it handing you everything.
- "Quiz me" covers step 4, explaining it back.
- "If you're unsure, say so" matters because AI can be wrong without sounding unsure. That's the next lesson.

You'll write your own version in the task below. Use your own words, because instructions you understand are instructions you'll actually use.
